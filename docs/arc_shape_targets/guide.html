A picture guide · ARC V3A magnet experiments

Shaping a star with magnets

Scientists want to make electricity the way the Sun makes light: by squeezing tiny particles together until they join. To do that on Earth you need gas that is hotter than the middle of the Sun, and nothing can touch it. So we hold it with magnets. This guide shows two small experiments we ran on a computer, and what we found out.

Chapter 1

A star in a magnet bottle

The machine is called ARC. It is shaped like a giant doughnut. Imagine cutting the doughnut in half and looking at the cut side. That's what the picture shows.

The glowing orange D is the super-hot gas, called plasma. The grey line is the inside wall of the machine. The coloured boxes are big magnets.

Each magnet pushes and pulls on the plasma. Together they decide its shape. A computer program called TokaMaker works out how hard each magnet has to pull to give the shape we ask for.

Chapter 2

Corners, exhaust lanes and landing spots

The plasma's edge isn't smooth all the way round. At the top and bottom it has a sharp corner. Scientists call it an X-point, because the magnetic lines cross there like an X.

From each corner, invisible exhaust lanes run down to the wall. Heat and used-up particles slide along them and land on special tough tiles. Those are the landing spots.

The landing spots get really hot. In our design there is also a second, helper corner further out. Helper corners are part of the "advanced" designs we study: they can split the exhaust and spread the heat out, a bit like spreading butter thinly so no single spot gets too much.

Chapter 3 · Experiment 1

Can we make the D less pointy?

How pointy the D is has a name: triangularity. A big number means very pointy. At first we asked the computer for a pointiness of 0.65. Then we asked for 0.60, a bit rounder. We changed nothing else: same magnets, same plasma, and the same corners and landing spots.

Why so little? The corners are pinned in place, like the pegs of a tent hammered into the ground. The pointiness of a D comes mostly from where its corners are. With the corners pinned, the computer can only nudge the sides a little.

Even the starting shape only reached 0.587, not 0.65. A pointiness of 0.65 would need the corners about 4 cm further in. We pinned them at a spot that matches about 0.62.

Chapter 4 · The surprise

The magnet tug-of-war

The plasma barely changed shape, so you might expect the magnets to barely change too. They didn't behave like that! Some magnets flipped from pulling one way to pulling hard the other way.

Bars show total magnet current (millions of amp-turns). Right = one direction, left = the other. Top and bottom magnets are the same, so each pair is shown once.

So why didn't the plasma move?

Think of a tug-of-war. If two strong teams pull hard in opposite directions, the rope stays still. That's what happened: the magnet changes cancel each other out where the plasma is.

We checked by asking: "if only this one magnet pair changed, how much would the plasma's edge be pushed?" Then we let them all change together.

What this means: lots of different magnet settings give almost the same plasma. The computer picked one of them, but it could just as well have picked another. So big magnet changes here don't mean "rounder plasmas need much stronger magnets". To get trustworthy magnet numbers we need extra rules that tell the computer which setting to prefer.
Chapter 5 · Experiment 2

Moving the helper corner

Next we went back to pointiness 0.65 and moved only the helper corners 5 cm outward, about the width of your palm. Everything else stayed the same, including where we wanted the exhaust to land.

What happened: the helper corner went exactly where we asked. The outer exhaust lane moved with it, so its landing spot slid about 7 cm along the wall, further from where we wanted it. The main plasma didn't change at all, and the magnets only changed a little.

So the helper corner and the landing spot are linked. You can't choose them separately: the exhaust lands wherever the lane from the helper corner happens to hit the wall.

Chapter 6

What we learned

1. Pinned corners decide the shape

If the corners are pinned, asking for a different pointiness hardly does anything. To really change the shape, the corners have to move too.

2. "What you ask" isn't "what you get"

The computer balances all our wishes. When two wishes disagree, the more important one wins and the other gets a bit worse.

3. Magnets can cancel each other

Many magnet settings make the same plasma. We need better rules before we can say how hard the real magnets must work.

4. Helper corners steer the exhaust

Moving a helper corner moves where the exhaust lands. That's exactly the kind of control advanced designs want, but it has to be planned together.

Still a mystery (next steps)

  • If we move the corners together with the pointiness, can the magnets really change the shape?
  • What are the real limits of ARC's magnets? The limits in this test were far too generous to tell us.
  • Does everything still work when the plasma heats up and changes over time? (That's the next project, with a program called TORAX.)

The real numbers

Static free-boundary TokaMaker equilibria (OFT 26.9) with fixed plasma profiles. Ip = 12 MA. Each case changes one input from the baseline. Full write-up: REPORT.md.

"Pointiness" is triangularity δ as reported by TokaMaker's get_stats. Magnet currents are total MA-turns. "Edge push" is the spread of coil-only vacuum flux along the boundary targets (Wb/rad). The setup (targets, weights, coil bounds, regularization) comes from F. Sheehan's ARC V3A shape-testing notebook; the mesh is the public OFT ARC V3A mesh.
